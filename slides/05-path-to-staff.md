# Building Your Network

Everything else is contingent on understanding the wider company, understanding hurdles outside your team, and having the right contacts to get involved when opportunity presents itself.

- Develop relationships across teams **before you need them**
- Attend architecture reviews and planning meetings outside your team
- Make yourself known as someone who helps and unblocks others
- Build bridges between teams and departments

<!--
At a senior level you likely don't have the access that comes with the staff title yet, so you need to start backrooming the conversations to get enough awareness of the business and the people who are the key members of the organisation. You also only have your personal reputation to put behind your efforts, so make yourself the face of the systems you manage so key members in other teams know you.
If your company has donut chats or something that can help you meet people, lean into that. A casual chat about some tech 6 months ago can make an emerging planning meeting between your team and another go much more smoothly and set you up for success.
Make sure you're getting to do skip levels. You want a communication pathway that lets you ask questions of people closer to the top and hear about whats concerning them.

When an important cross-team problem comes up, you want people to think of you. That only happens if they already know you.
When another team does show up asking for help, dive into offering it. Pull out all the stops being the person who went the extra mile to get that team unblocked today instead of next week.
-->

# Finding Staff-Level Work

**Expand Your Impact Radius:**

- Take on problems that affect multiple teams
- Volunteer for cross-team initiatives and working groups
- Look for where your team's work intersects with others

**Problem Discovery:**
- Don't wait for assignments - identify important problems
- Surface technical debt, risks, and opportunities
- Practice connecting technical problems to business impact

<!--
You need to start acting like a Staff engineer before you have the title.
You're not going to have the same freedom and autonomy that is offered at Staff, so you need to find problems that can align with your teams objectives. When they pop up, get your manager onboard with the idea of you solving it. They might still have to fit on your teams roadmap, but it can be you who makes the change happen.
And as much as possible ground these changes in the business impact that they will have.
-->

# Developing the Skills

**Seek Mentorship:**
- Find Staff+ engineers to mentor you
- Shadow them in design reviews and cross-team meetings
- Get feedback on your technical vision and communication

<!--
The other big thing that needs to come out of your network is finding mentors. If you can learn from them instead of figuring it out yourself, you'll improve much faster.
Don't be afraid to formalise these mentorship relationships when you find them. Weekly or fortnightly catchups where you go over the challenges of the last week and see where you can get assistance to move forward can be invaluable.
These sort of relationships can also position you such that you're dragged into more important work. If yours is the first name that comes to mind when a staff plus needs to delegate in your area, opportunities will start coming to you.
-->

---

# Developing the Skills

**Develop Social Mastery:**
- Practice building consensus on technical decisions
- Learn to influence without authority
- Work on written communication: RFCs, design docs
- Study how decisions really get made in your org

<!--
You need to practice the social parts of the role and step outside of your comfort zone.
Within reason, start helping technical decisions reach consensus without being the one to design them, get used to letting go of control a bit and seeing how that can still be successful. Do more of your work in the open and see how other engineers challenge your process or engage with it and find those patterns that work best and those which lead to dead ends.

And you need to become more politically aware about why things happen in your organisation. Your project has been proceeding fine and then one day your manager turns up with a pivot, you want to dig deeper to know what conversation occurred to cause this. Is this fallout from a demo and discussion with a director? Was it a late realisation because stakeholders weren't paying enough attention during earlier planning? Did someone on the other side of the company get roasted in public for making a similar mistake and now your manager is seeing the risk? Knowing whether these changes come from being risk adverse, or proactive improvement can dramatically change how you should address them and what options you have available to you.
-->

# Showing Up at the Next Level

**Demonstrate the Behaviors:**
- Take ownership beyond your assigned work
- Lead by example on quality and standards
- Make others effective through unblocking and mentoring

**Become Indispensable:**
- Be the person peers turn to for guidance
- Build a reputation: "we need to get [your name] involved"
- Be the engineer others can't imagine the org without

<!--
You need to be showing up like a staff engineer before you get the promotion.

Stretch ownership beyond where you have previously to go deeper into the tech stack or further into the boundaries of neighbouring solutions. Your endpoints are part of an internal platform, be the one who's looking for anti-patterns from the teams consuming your work and be ready to help resolve those.
If there's an area your team has been struggling with, get yourself assigned to them. Observability has been confusing and your SLOs are feeling meaningless, start trying to rework it all. It will help your team perform better, but it's the sort of work that's visible multiple levels above you.

The reputation that comes from this investment will start paying off. You'll be the person that the movers and shakers come to when they have something that needs solving, and you'll have engineers coming to you for guidance. Overtime this saves you from having to directly invest in networking and building relationships because the right people around the company start coming to you.
-->

# Making Your Case

**Document Your Impact:**
- Track cross-team impact, not just individual contributions
- Note when you've unblocked teams or influenced direction
- Collect feedback from peers, other teams, and leadership

**Communicate Your Readiness:**
- Have explicit conversations with your manager
- Understand what "good" looks like at Staff in your org
- Seek feedback on gaps and work to close them
- Make your Staff-level work visible to decision-makers

<!--
Finally you need to build your promo pack.
As you're changing the way you work and finding ways to make more impact, you need to document it. Have a list of your greatest hits, and it doesn't hurt to capture your biggest flops either. Given how staff level impact can take quarters to pay off, you want to look for the final outcomes the other side of your investments. You renovated the bug triage process in March, it won't be enough to just call out this work. It'll take a few months for that process to highlight the most important bugs and for them to be addressed by the engineers, but by August you'll probably be able to measure a reduction in outstanding critical issues or a reduction in cycle time from user reports. That it had measurable impacts on business outcomes and that you were able to correlate that outcome to your change is a much stronger indicator that you're operating at the next level.

You also need to plan this with your manager and probably the next level above them as well. For starters you still have a regular Senior's workload and you need to find capacity and opportunities to perform this more impactful work without hurting yours or your teams regular velocity, your manager can help you with this. You want their regular feedback and you need to be seen to be taking it onboard and developing your skills around it. And you need them to be recognising your impact because come promotion time, they will be the ones that need to go and advocate for you.
-->

# Timeline & Expectations

- This transition **doesn't happen organically** - it requires deliberate effort

- Expect **1+ years** of investment into showing up at the next level

- This is ON TOP OF years at Senior to build your foundation

- Be patient but intentional - this is a long-term investment

<!--
There's no shortcut here. Staff is a significant step up, and it takes real time and effort to get there.
Unlike moving to senior, it's not as simple as getting better at what you were already doing and then there being a good chance someone else will recognise you for the promotion. There's fewer seats at the staff level and they require different business justification than simply having a more senior engineering workforce.

Having your manager say you'll be ready in 6 months does not mean there's a position available in 6 months. You might be stuck waiting for someone to exit the company or an expansion or re-org to create a new requirement for leadership.
When that opportunity does come around, you want to be the obvious and un-controversial choice. The thought from the decision makers should be that you're already doing this work anyway.

You need to be a good senior engineer first, so if you fee like you're still working to perform well within that role, focus on that first, but once you switch focus to chasing staff, expect that to be at least a year of pursuing it.
